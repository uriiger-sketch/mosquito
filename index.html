          About MosquitoNet

          Hear the mosquito
          before it bites.

          MosquitoNet turns a phone into an acoustic mosquito detector. It listens for the wingbeat tone each species makes, names the likely species on the device, and adds confirmed finds — anonymously — to a global map of disease vectors used by households, field teams and researchers.

          On-device analysis No audio stored Anonymous by design Published science

          How detection works12 species · two detectors

          Every mosquito species beats its wings at a characteristic rate — a faint, steady tone between about 290 and 730 Hz, with overtones at two and three times that pitch. MosquitoNet computes a high-resolution spectrum of the room many times a second and looks for narrow, sustained tones with exactly that harmonic structure.

          Frequency priors come from two open datasets: HumBugDB (Kiskin et al. 2021 — 20 hours, 36 species, CC-BY-4.0) and the Abuzz dataset (Mukundarajan et al. 2017, eLife).

          Species MosquitoNet can recognise

          Two detectors run on every frame

          • Close range — a filter bank tuned to each species’ wingbeat, with signal-to-noise, tonality, periodicity and harmonic-ratio checks. Very selective; works for mosquitoes close to the phone.
          • Long range — a harmonic track-before-detect tracker borrowed from sonar. Each frequency is compared with its own noise floor, evidence from f0, 2f0 and 3f0 is combined, and weak frames accumulate along the drifting pitch for about 1.5 s. Voices and motors are rejected by physics: nothing may sound below the fundamental, and perfectly steady tones (fans, motors) are notched out. Detections it makes are labelled Far.
          • A tone must persist across most of a ~2-second window before anything is shown. Only finds that reach 70% confidence are shared with the global map.

          Getting the most rangePlacement and room tips

          A wingbeat is very quiet. Beyond about 10 cm its sound halves (−6 dB) each time the distance doubles, so range is set by how far the tone stands above background noise — every 6 dB you win back doubles the distance.

          • Go where the mosquitoes go. They home in on breath and body heat. Put the phone beside the sleeper’s head, 20–40 cm away; halving the distance gains 6 dB.
          • Lay the phone flat on a hard surface — table, floor or bed frame — microphone toward the sleeping area. A hard boundary adds about 3 dB.
          • Switch off fans and air-conditioning. They add broadband noise and blow away the CO2 plume that draws mosquitoes in.
          • Keep the room quiet — no TV, music or conversation near the phone.
          • Remove the phone case if it partly covers the microphone.
          • Timing: dusk and through the night for Anopheles and Culex; daytime for Aedes.

          About the map dataSources and cleaning

          Each circle is one documented location where the vector is present — never a synthetic or random point. Colour shows the species; ringed circles mark invasive first detections (for example An. stephensi in Africa); dashed circles mark detected or introduced populations that are not established.

          Every record is checked against a 1:10m land mask (points in the sea or in lakes are corrected or removed) and de-duplicated. The records, their sources and the cleaning steps are rebuilt reproducibly by tools/build_map_data.py.

          • WHO World Malaria Report 2025 and Sinka et al. 2012 (dominant Anopheles vectors)
          • PAHO dengue bulletins 2024, CDC ArboNET, ECDC invasive-mosquito maps (June 2025)
          • Kraemer et al. 2015 global Aedes compendium (eLife, doi:10.7554/eLife.08347)
          • An. stephensi invasion: Djibouti 2012, Kebri Dehar (Ethiopia) 2016, Arba Minch, Marsabit, Turkana and Kisumu (Kenya), Accra (Ghana) 2022, Al Hudaydah (Yemen) 2021, Jaffna (Sri Lanka) 2017

          The year control is cumulative: “up to 2010” shows everything documented by 2010. Community detections appear as squares once a 5 km zone collects enough confirmed finds.

          Privacy & federated learningWhat leaves your phone — and what never does
          • No audio is stored or transmitted. Microphone data is analysed in real time and immediately discarded.
          • What does leave the device: confirmed detections (species, genus, confidence, wingbeat frequency, signal strength, and a location randomly shifted by up to ~2.8 km), and differentially private model weights for federated learning.
          • Your history stays here. The History tab is stored only in this browser; export or clear it at any time.
          • Differential privacy: Gaussian mechanism with ε = 1.0, δ = 10−5; gradients are clipped to an L2 norm of 1.0 before noise is added.
          • Anonymous device ID: a random 128-bit value created on your device and never linked to your identity. The server stores only its SHA-256 hash.
          • Federated averaging: each device uploads its locally trained weights every 20 training steps; the server averages them, weighted by step count.

          MosquitoNet · A citizen-science tool, not a medical device.

          Hear the mosquito
          before it bites.

          MosquitoNet listens for the wingbeat tones of disease-carrying mosquitoes and tells you which species is in the room.

          • It listens for wingbeats

            Each species hums at its own pitch. The detector picks out that tone and names the likely species — malaria, dengue or West Nile carriers among them.

          • Private by design

            Sound is analysed on this phone and immediately discarded — nothing is recorded or uploaded. Confirmed detections are shared anonymously, with location blurred by about 3 km.

          • Place it by the bed

            Lay the phone flat, 20–40 cm from the sleeper’s head, and switch off fans. The screen dims itself while it listens.

          You’ll also be asked for location, used only to pick the species found in your region. It’s optional.

          Microphone access is off

          MosquitoNet needs the microphone to hear wingbeats. Sound is analysed on this device and never recorded. Turn access back on, then try again:

          iPhone or iPad
          1. Tap aA in Safari’s address bar, then Website Settings.
          2. Set Microphone to Allow.
          3. Or open Settings → Safari → Microphone and choose Allow.
          Android
          1. Tap the icon left of the address bar, then Permissions.
          2. Turn on Microphone.
          3. If it stays blocked: Settings → Apps → Chrome → Permissions → Microphone → Allow.
          Computer
          1. Click the site-settings icon in the address bar and set Microphone to Allow.
          2. Reload the page.
          3. On macOS, also check System Settings → Privacy & Security → Microphone.

          Location access is off

          Location is optional. It lets MosquitoNet focus on the species found in your region and place your anonymous detections on the map (blurred by about 3 km). Detection works without it.

          iPhone or iPad
          1. Open Settings → Privacy & Security → Location Services and make sure it is on.
          2. Scroll to Safari Websites and choose While Using the App.
          3. Return here and tap Try again.
          Android
          1. Tap the icon left of the address bar, then Permissions → Location → Allow.
          2. Check that device location is on in quick settings.
          Computer
          1. Click the site-settings icon in the address bar and set Location to Allow.
          2. Reload the page.

          Clear detection history?
